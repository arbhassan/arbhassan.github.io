Price is an opinion. Value is an argument. Fundamental analysis is the discipline of building that argument from the ground up — interest rates, inflation, earnings, liquidity, and the behaviour of institutions — then asking whether the market's opinion is wrong.

01 — Foundations

Four lenses that explain almost everything

Fundamental analysis is not one thing. It's four overlapping lenses, and every meaningful move in gold or the Nasdaq can be traced back to at least one of them.

◎

Macro

Growth, employment, inflation, PMIs, credit conditions. The backdrop that sets the rules of the game for every other asset class.

⌘

Monetary

Policy rates, real yields, central bank balance sheets, forward guidance. The price of money is the price of everything.

▤

Fundamental

For equities: revenue, margins, cash flow, return on capital. For gold: mine supply, official-sector demand, physical offtake.

⇄

Flows & Positioning

ETF holdings, futures positioning, central bank reserves, buybacks. Fundamentals set direction; flows set timing.

The core question is always the same: what is this asset worth, given the current and expected path of interest rates, growth, and risk? Everything else is detail.

02 — Gold

A rock with no cash flow — and that's the point

Gold pays no coupon, no dividend, and has no earnings. So its value must be derived entirely from opportunity cost, currency debasement, and fear. That makes its fundamental drivers unusually clean.

01

Real interest rates Inverse

The single most important variable. Gold's "yield" is zero, so what matters is the real yield — nominal yield minus inflation expectations. When 10-year TIPS yields fall, holding gold costs less versus holding bonds, and bullion rallies. When real yields spike, gold bleeds.

02

The US dollar Inverse

Gold is quoted in dollars globally. A stronger dollar makes gold more expensive for every non-US buyer, suppressing demand. A weaker dollar does the opposite. This is why dollar indices and gold often look like mirror images.

03

Inflation & debasement Nuanced

Gold is a hedge against unexpected inflation and against the loss of confidence in fiat currency — not against measured CPI. If inflation rises but the Fed hikes faster, real yields rise and gold can fall anyway. Context decides.

04

Official sector demand Structural

Central bank buying — particularly from China, Russia, India, Turkey and Poland — has become the dominant structural bid since 2022. This is price-insensitive, reserve-driven demand. It doesn't care about the Fed. It cares about sanctions risk and de-dollarisation.

05

Geopolitics & risk Positive

War, sanctions, sovereign default risk and banking stress push capital into assets with no counterparty. Gold is the only liquid asset that is nobody's liability.

06

ETF flows & positioning Amplifier

Western ETF holdings are momentum-following and rate-sensitive. Asian physical demand is price-sensitive and bargain-hunting. The tension between the two creates gold's characteristic choppy range behaviour.

Gold price US 10Y real yield (inverted)

The mirror. When real yields fall (red line moves down on this inverted axis), gold rises — and vice versa. The relationship isn't perfect, and the 2022–2024 gap is exactly where central bank buying broke the correlation.

Gold doesn't have earnings. It has a cost of carry — and a cost of trust.

03 — Nasdaq

A claim on the future, discounted back to today

Every Nasdaq stock is worth the present value of its future cash flows. That single sentence contains all of its fundamental drivers: how much cash, how far in the future, and what discount rate.

Nasdaq 100 earnings power Policy rate path

The tug of war. Nasdaq earnings compound upward over time, but the discount rate yanks the valuation multiple around violently. In 2022 the rate spike won. From 2023 the earnings line won — driven almost entirely by AI infrastructure spending.

01

Interest rates & the discount rate Dominant

Nasdaq cash flows are weighted heavily toward the distant future. The further out a cash flow sits, the more a change in the discount rate hurts it. A 1% move in long yields can wipe 15–25% off a high-multiple software name and barely touch a utility. This is why the Nasdaq is the purest expression of rate expectations in equity markets.

02

Earnings growth & revisions Positive

The other half of the equation. Revenue growth, operating leverage, margins, and — most importantly — the direction of analyst revisions. Markets price the second derivative. Accelerating growth gets a premium multiple; decelerating growth gets compressed, even if absolute earnings still rise.

03

Liquidity & the Fed balance sheet Positive

QE, reserve balances, reverse repo drains and Treasury issuance composition all change how much cash is chasing risk assets. Liquidity is the tide; the Nasdaq is the boat with the tallest mast.

04

Risk appetite & credit spreads Regime

When credit spreads widen and volatility spikes, duration-heavy equities are sold first. When spreads compress, they're bought first. This is the risk-on / risk-off switch, and it moves faster than any earnings report.

05

Concentration & the AI capex cycle Structural

A handful of mega-caps now drive the majority of the index. Their capital expenditure on compute, data centres and power is itself a revenue stream for other index members. This creates a self-reinforcing loop — powerful on the way up, fragile on the way down.

06

The US dollar Mild negative

A large share of Nasdaq revenue is earned overseas. A strong dollar is a translation headwind to reported earnings — a second-order but persistent drag.

Gold is priced off what money is worth. The Nasdaq is priced off what money will earn.

06 — Process

How to actually do it

A repeatable workflow beats a clever opinion. Five steps, in order.

01

Define the regime first

Before looking at any single asset, classify the macro regime: is growth accelerating or decelerating? Is inflation above or below target? Is the central bank tightening or easing? Gold and the Nasdaq behave completely differently in each of the four quadrants.

02

Identify the dominant driver

At any moment, one variable is doing most of the work. In 2022 it was real yields. In 2024 for gold it was central bank buying; for the Nasdaq it was AI earnings. Find the variable that explains the most variance, and anchor your thesis to it.

03

Build the valuation anchor

For gold: real yields, dollar, and the size of the official-sector bid. For the Nasdaq: earnings estimates, growth durability, and a defensible discount rate. You need a number — or at least a range — that says "fair."

04

Check positioning & flows

Fundamentals tell you direction; positioning tells you how much of it is already priced. Crowded longs amplify downside; capitulation lows set up asymmetric upside. Look at ETF holdings, futures positioning, and options skew.

05

Define what would prove you wrong

The mark of a real analyst isn't conviction — it's falsifiability. Write down the specific data points that would invalidate the thesis. If you can't name them, you don't have a thesis, you have a feeling.
